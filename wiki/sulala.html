    Sulala

    Sulala

    Show on the map

    As the capital of Abu, Sulala stands as one of the wealthiest trade nexuses on Athura. The city is defined by its four rings, each a social stratum within itself.

    • Outer Ring
    • Second Ring
    • Third Ring
    • Center

    Outer Ring

    Within the outer ring, the largest and most densely populated district, homes double as storefronts; storefronts spill into the streets. Clay ovens burn from dawn until deep night. Spiced stews, roasted grains, skewered meats, and sugared fruits perfume the air. Pottery and hammered metalwork are displayed openly, as much an advertisement as a declaration of pride. Prosperity and desperation coexist here. With trade comes opportunity; with opportunity comes theft. Crime is common. Sulaladji guards patrol heavily, especially near the city gates. Enforcement is visible, deliberate, and occasionally brutal. Children run unsupervised through the tight alleys. Ringo porters weave between atlae merchants with loaded carts. The noise never truly stops.

    Second Ring

    Within the second ring, the chaos thins into organization. Inns, clinics, apothecaries, jewelers, and large trade houses operate in stone buildings with proper façades and signage. Contracts are written here. Debts are negotiated. Alliances are forged over tea rather than shouted over bargaining tables. Travelers who can afford comfort rarely sleep in the outer ring. Information brokers and merchant consortia quietly shape Abu’s economy from this layer. This is also where Sulalas influence becomes subtle rather than loud.

    Third Ring

    The third ring contains wide streets, controlled greenery, and a wealthy citizenry. They reside here behind sculpted walls and ornamental gates. Prestigious academies educate the children of merchant dynasties and political elites. Gardens are carefully maintained despite the encroaching desert. Water management systems are hidden beneath marble and tile — a quiet flex of engineering dominance over the Wastes. Crime is nearly nonexistent. Not because morality improves inward, but because protection does.

    Center

    At the center lie the palatial grounds: Yidji Palace, administrative halls, and ceremonial plazas. Access is restricted. Guards do not merely patrol; they perform visibility. Captains and lieutenants conduct marches and training exercises in open courtyards. Discipline is meant to be seen. This ring is less a district and more a statement: order radiates outward from here.

    Sulala’s design makes it stable, but fragile in a specific way. Revolt would not begin at Yidji Palace. It would begin in the outer ring, where density, economic pressure, and resentment overlap. Should instability ignite, it would move inward like a tightening vice. So as a countermeasure, the gates of each ring gate are engineered to withstand siege. Collapsible bridges span defensive moats. Oiled stones are stockpiled above gate-bays, ready to be ignited and cast downward. Arrow slits line interior corridors to allow defenders to fire into the gate passage itself.
