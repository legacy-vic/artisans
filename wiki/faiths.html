    Faiths

    The two great faiths of Artisans: the Faith of the Hidden Ones, dominant in Athura, and the Order of the Ancients.

    The Faith of the Hidden Ones

    The Faith of the Hidden Ones is the dominant Athuran religion centered on the conviction that the gods are real, active, and omnipresent, yet rarely, if ever, perceivable in direct form. The pantheon is collectively called “The Hidden Ones” or “Those Who Remain Unseen,” a phrase meant literally. Temples and roadside shrines are civic necessities as much as spiritual ones. They are places to offer thanks, avert harm, and acknowledge the forces that govern life.

    Religion in the Faith of the Hidden Ones is treated as a relationship of distance and consequence rather than intimacy and constant reassurance. Mortals do not assume the gods are obligated to comfort them; they assume the gods will act according to domain and temperament. Prayer is therefore context-driven—asked when needed, offered when spared, repeated when fear demands a name—and it is culturally acceptable to address certain gods with reverent anger or frank distrust, especially Oiyas, because Death is seen as necessary but not tender. The faithful aim less to “understand” the gods than to live in ways that do not invite their worst expressions: to honor Adena without indulging excess, to respect Oiyas without pretending to love him, to appease Phera when the sea turns sharp, and to keep shrines tended so the unseen never feels ignored.

    A common opening invocation frames this posture of reverence without presumption:

    “Hidden Ones, who move without footstep and speak without tongue—see us, though we cannot see you.”

    Adena is revered as the 'Goddess of Life,' governing the living balance of ecosystems; prayers to her often sound like gratitude paired with restraint.

    “Oh, Adena. The beauty of your forests from which all life began. You are that which all strive to be, though we cannot.”

    Oiyas is the 'God of Death' and keeper of the Da’et, feared not as evil but as finality, necessity, and impartial ending.

    “Before us is the God who dared sully Adena, his lover, beautiful and perfect. Oiyas, protector of the sunken realm, the Da’et… I serve you, yet I do not trust you… Hear me, oh Great Oiyas.”

    Rhodite, the 'God of Skirmish,' governs righteous conflict, honorable defense, and guardianship—invoked by soldiers and city-guards who want victory without becoming monsters.

    “Rhodite, set my feet true and my hand steady; let me strike only what I must.”

    Phera, the 'Goddess of Weather,' governs her domain with the volatile temperament of sea and sky; sailors speak to her less like a beloved and more like a sovereign to be appeased.

    “Phera, spare this hull; take our offering and let your wrath pass over open water.”

    Artisia, 'Goddess of Land and Planets,' governs Artisans’ stability, geological shift, tides, and the slow order of celestial movement; farmers and builders ask for steadiness rather than miracles.

    “Artisia, hold the ground beneath us—let the world keep its shape.”

    Cezolis, 'Goddess of the Sun,' governs day and night, seasons, warmth, and the destructive edge of heat.

    “Cezolis, burn bright but not bitter; give warmth to seed and skin.”

    Idu, 'God of Time and Innovation,' governs the cyclical nature of life, destiny, learning, and the push toward invention; scholars and artisans ask for progress that doesn’t become hubris.

    “Idu, let my hands make what my mind can bear; let my work outlive my error.”

    Shakir, 'God of Wealth & Prosperity,' governs prosperity and good fortune, but is traditionally approached through generosity rather than hoarding.

    “Shakir, let abundance pass through me—full hands, open hands.”
