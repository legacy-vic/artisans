    Order of the Ancients

    The Order of the Ancients is the dominant religion of Icos and one of the principal faiths practiced within territories influenced by Icosi culture. Followers of the Order worship a defined pantheon of deities known collectively as the Ancients, whom they believe once physically inhabited the mortal world and lived among the civilizations of antiquity.

    • Principles

    According to the Order, the Ancients were not mortals who achieved divinity, but beings who were divine from their beginning. They created and shaped the world in successive ages, established many of the foundations upon which mortal civilization rests, and eventually passed from the mortal realm into domains of their own. From these domains, the Ancients are believed to continue influencing the world.

    The faith is closely intertwined with the hierarchical society of Icos. Hereditary rule, inherited responsibilities, oaths, ancestry, and continuity between generations are regarded as parts of the natural order of mortal civilization. Royal blood in particular is traditionally viewed as spiritually nearer to the gods, even that noble birth alone does not make an individual pious.

    The Order is not entirely static. Its teachings develop through public sermons and the circumstances of different historical periods, with figures and events of contemporary importance influencing how the stories of the Ancients are told and understood.

    The gods themselves possess familial relationships, and divine lineage contributes to a hierarchy within the pantheon. This distinction between divine and mortal birth forms one of the central theological boundaries of the Order. A god may be born from gods, whether through procreation or other divine means. A mortal cannot become a god merely through accomplishment, authority, wealth, or reputation. To claim otherwise is regarded by orthodox believers as among the gravest offenses a mortal can commit.

    Orthodoxy

    Orthodox teachings maintain an absolute distinction between the divine and the mortal. The Ancients were gods because they were ordained to be. Their accomplishments demonstrate their nature but did not create it. A mortal performing extraordinary deeds may earn renown, authority, or remembrance, but cannot thereby make themselves divine.

    Feigning godhood is consequently considered an extreme act of sacrilege. To compare oneself directly to an Ancient is to claim a nature one does not possess and to place oneself alongside the beings responsible for the creation and ordering of the world. Several denominations have disputed this interpretation, however. These groups teach that mortals who perform sufficiently extraordinary feats may attain a state comparable to godhood. The orthodox Order rejects such teachings as heresy.

    Creation

    The Order teaches that the formation of the world occurred through a succession of divine acts.

    • Artisia first created the world itself and the celestial bodies surrounding it.
    • Cezolis then gave the world light.
    • Adena brought life into creation, while Oies introduced death.
    • With life and death established, Haethum gave mortals the tools necessary to create civilization.
    • Much later, Lucan and Millera introduced mortals to a more ordered and prosperous form of governance.
      This progression gives the pantheon a loose chronology within religious tradition. The deeds of one Ancient establish the conditions necessary for those who follow: a world must exist before it can be illuminated, life must exist before it can die, and mortals must exist before civilization can be built.
      Lucan and Millera occupy a particularly important place within Icosi political theology because of their association with rulership. The two are believed to have jointly ruled the ancient civilization of Azkassar, where their subjects regarded them as gods as well as kings. Their kingdom possessed wealth considered unimaginable for its age and was remembered as both prosperous and unassailable, without the decadence that followers might otherwise associate with unchecked luxury. Their reign serves as one of the Order's principal models for mortal governance.

    The Nine Domains

    The nine Ancients are believed to possess individual domains beyond the mortal realm.

    When an Ancient dies, they pass into their domain and continue to exist there eternally. From these domains, the Ancients remain capable of influencing mortal affairs. Prayer is therefore not merely commemorative. Followers may petition the Ancients for intervention while also venerating them for the deeds they performed during their existence in the mortal world. The exact nature of such intervention is understood through faith, stories, and religious interpretation rather than through any universally defined set of rules.

    Afterlife

    Followers of the Order are encouraged to choose a patron Ancient during their mortal lives.

    A devout follower who faithfully serves their chosen deity is believed to enter that Ancient's domain after death. There they may continue serving their patron for eternity. Those who reject the gods, lack sufficient devotion, or otherwise die without such favor are instead believed to pass into the keeping of Oies.

    Oies rules the Realm of the Scorned, where the condemned labor as slaves upon Korkophilopolis, his city-temple. The city is said to be without completion, ensuring that the dead consigned there may toil upon it forever. The fear of Oies therefore concerns more than death itself. He represents the fate awaiting those who enter death without the protection or favor of another Ancient.

    Ancestor Veneration

    Ancestor veneration is common among followers of the Order. The dead are remembered as previous custodians of the obligations, names, lands, and traditions inherited by the living. Ancestors who held royal titles receive particular reverence and may be regarded as having drawn closer to the gods after death.

    This reverence does not ordinarily make an ancestor an Ancient. The distinction between honored mortal and god remains fundamental to orthodox belief.

    Those known to have worshipped Oies during life are generally excluded from the same assumptions of divine closeness afforded to other prestigious ancestors.

    As a result, lineage within Icos possesses both political and spiritual importance. To dishonor one's ancestors is not merely to embarrass a family name, but to show contempt toward the generations whose responsibilities have been inherited by the living.

    Conduct

    The Order possesses no singular comprehensive code defining the behavior of a good follower. Instead, the stories of the Ancients provide examples, warnings, and cautionary tales from which followers are expected to draw lessons. Different individuals may emphasize different stories and different gods depending upon their circumstances and patronage. Certain actions nevertheless carry severe religious weight.

    Abandoning an inherited responsibility, breaking a sworn oath, violating guest right, dishonoring one's ancestors, and comparing oneself to the Ancients can all constitute profound moral failures. Their severity is not necessarily ranked universally. The weight placed upon each offense often depends upon the individual, their circumstances, their family, and the obligations they possess.

    Hierarchy & Society

    The Order considers hierarchy the natural state of mortal civilization.

    The progression of divine history—from creation, to life, to civilization, and eventually to the ordered rule represented by Lucan and Millera—is interpreted as evidence that structured society is part of the proper development of the mortal world.

    Icosi feudalism consequently exists comfortably alongside the teachings of the Order.

    Families inherit lands, titles, offices, obligations, and reputations from those who preceded them. An inherited responsibility is not considered solely a privilege belonging to its current holder, but something entrusted to them by previous generations and eventually owed to those who follow.

    Abandoning such a responsibility may therefore possess both social and religious implications.

    Royalty occupies a particularly elevated position. Because rulership represents the final stage of civilization associated with Lucan and Millera, royal blood is traditionally regarded as spiritually closer to the gods than that of the lowborn.

    This does not make every king, queen, prince, or noble virtuous.

    Rank and piety are distinct. A ruler may occupy a position nearer to the divine order while still proving themselves impious, dishonorable, or otherwise unworthy of the responsibilities inherited from their ancestors.

    Organization

    Priesthood

    The Order maintains an organized male priesthood. Only clergymen are permitted to enter the interior of its temples. Worshippers generally gather outside, where they kneel in prayer or assemble to hear sermons delivered by priests.

    Priests are forbidden from ordinary labor, hunting, and personal indulgence. Their material needs are instead supported by contributions from followers, most commonly food and money.

    This dependence allows the clergy to devote themselves wholly to religious life while making the support of the priesthood an ordinary expression of devotion among believers.

    Sermons

    Public sermons form an important part of the Order's religious life. Priests recount stories of the Ancients, interpret their deeds, offer warnings or lessons drawn from them, and may relate these teachings to circumstances affecting the society around them. Chroniclers are commonly hired to record what is said during important sermons.

    Because sermons participate in the continuing development of the faith, religious interpretation can change alongside the historical circumstances in which the Order exists. The pantheon remains defined, but the prominence afforded to particular stories, Ancients, and interpretations may shift over time.

    Temples

    Temples of the Order are enormous structures, commonly comparable in scale to castles and royal palaces. Their size reflects both the influence of the faith and the reverence afforded to the Ancients.

    The interior is reserved for the clergy and houses objects of religious significance, including relics attributed to the gods themselves. Ordinary worship instead takes place outside the temple, where large gatherings may assemble before its entrance for prayer and sermons.

    The specific architectural and visual traditions distinguishing these temples from secular Icosi buildings vary from region to region and are not universally defined.

    Practices

    Prayer

    Most adherents pray privately. Particularly devout adherents may regularly travel to a local temple to kneel among other worshippers and listen to public sermons. Followers may also bring offerings of food or money to sustain the clergy.

    The orthodox Order does not require constant public demonstrations of devotion for a person to be considered a follower.

    Patronage

    Followers may dedicate themselves to one Ancient as their patron deity.

    Such patronage carries significance beyond mortal life, as faithful service is believed to determine the domain into which a believer passes after death. The choice of patron does not require a follower to deny or reject the other Ancients.

    Birth

    Priests frequently oversee births. Their presence places the beginning of mortal life beneath the observation of a faith whose creation tradition attributes life itself to Adena.

    Marriage

    Marriage ceremonies are commonly overseen by priests. The precise rites involved may differ, but clerical participation places the union within the religious traditions of the Order.

    Death

    Priests oversee funeral pyres and the rites surrounding the dead. Death carries particular significance because the deceased is believed to pass either into the domain of their patron or, if found without divine favor, into the Realm of the Scorned.

    Coronation

    Religious involvement in coronations varies between families and regions, but the Order participates more often than not.

    A ruler maintaining visible good standing with the faith is regarded favorably by both worshippers and clergy, and religious participation reinforces the association between rulership and the divine order represented by Lucan and Millera. On particularly significant occasions, relics attributed to the Ancients may be removed from temples and incorporated into coronation rites.

    Birthdays may likewise involve the Order according to regional or familial preference, though religious participation is not universal.

    Relics

    Objects believed to have belonged to or been associated with the Ancients are preserved within temples.

    Such relics are rarely taken outside. Exceptions may be made for events of exceptional religious or political significance, particularly major coronations and funerals. Relics associated with Lucan and Millera hold obvious significance during ceremonies involving royalty, though objects associated with other Ancients may also be used when considered appropriate.

    The rarity with which these objects leave their temples contributes to their importance when they are displayed publicly.

    Heresy

    Disagreement within the Order is not automatically met with persecution. Competing interpretations and theological discourse may persist so long as they remain limited enough not to challenge the broader authority of the established faith. The most significant divide concerns mortal divinity.

    Certain denominations believe extraordinary mortals possess the capacity to become god-like through accomplishments comparable to those attributed to the Ancients. Orthodox doctrine regards this belief as fundamentally incompatible with the divine nature of the gods. As long as such beliefs remain relatively marginal, they may exist primarily as subjects of dispute.

    When a heretical movement gains sufficient influence to become a rival institution or sect, the response may become political. The Order may call upon the crown to suppress the movement, with punishments ranging from imprisonment to execution. The matter therefore becomes most dangerous when theological dissent acquires enough followers, wealth, or influence to challenge established religious authority.

    Other Faiths

    The Order generally has little interest in the private beliefs of foreigners who live beyond its political and religious sphere. This tolerance becomes considerably narrower where authority is concerned.

    Those who seek titles or positions of influence within societies dominated by the Order are expected to exist within its religious framework. Foreign belief becomes more contentious when it intersects with political legitimacy.

    Attitudes toward competing religions also differ among sects and individuals. Adherents of the Faith of the Hidden Ones, the dominant tradition throughout much of Athura, are commonly regarded by followers of the Ancients as misguided and intellectually inferior for their beliefs.

    More fervent elements of the Order go further, viewing followers of the Hidden Ones as people who knowingly turn their backs upon the gods. Such sects may pursue conversion aggressively and, in extreme circumstances, resort to violence against competing faiths. These attitudes are not universal throughout the Order.

    The Order & the Crown

    The Order and the secular rulers of Icos occupy separate but closely connected positions within society. The faith provides theological legitimacy to hereditary rule by treating hierarchy as the natural condition of civilized mortals. In turn, rulers may offer protection and political support to the established religious order.

    A monarch benefits from being seen in good standing with the clergy, particularly during coronations and other ceremonies intended to establish legitimacy before both subjects and ancestors.

    The relationship is not without tension. The Order may appeal to secular rulers when religious disputes threaten its authority, while rulers must contend with a faith capable of shaping public perceptions of honor, ancestry, legitimacy, and proper governance. Neither institution can easily disregard the other.

    Principles

    Although the Order possesses no singular moral code, several expectations recur throughout its traditions:

    • Honor inherited responsibilities: What is received from one's predecessors should not be discarded without cause.
    • Keep one's oaths: A sworn obligation carries moral and social weight beyond personal convenience.
    • Respect guest right: Hospitality establishes obligations between host and guest which should not be casually violated.
    • Honor one's ancestors: The living inherit more than property from the dead.
    • Recognize the distinction between mortal and divine: Greatness does not make a mortal an Ancient.
    • Respect rightful hierarchy: Ordered ranks and inherited obligations are regarded as part of civilized existence.
      How these principles are prioritized depends heavily upon the believer. For one follower, breaking an oath may represent the worst stain upon a person's character. For another, abandoning a family responsibility or disgracing one's ancestors may be more shameful. What unites them is the belief that a mortal is not an isolated individual, but someone situated within obligations inherited from the past and carried toward the future.
